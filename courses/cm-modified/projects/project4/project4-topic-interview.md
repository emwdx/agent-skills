# Project 4 — Topic Selection Interview Guide

**For teacher use. Not for student.**

The goal of this conversation is to leave with two things locked in: a specific person the student will talk to, and a general challenge or problem that person faces. Everything else in the project flows from those two decisions.

Keep the conversation loose. The questions below are prompts, not a script. Follow his interest wherever it goes — the job is just to make sure you land on something concrete before he leaves.

---

## Before the conversation

Have a piece of paper or a shared doc open. When he says something that sounds like a real answer, write it down and read it back to him. He may not recognize his own good ideas without that confirmation.

---

## Opening — Warm him up

Start here to get him talking. Don't push for a "correct" answer yet.

- "So you finished the earthquake project. What do you remember most about it — what was the most interesting part?"
- "Was there anything about the earthquake project that felt boring or that you didn't care about as much?"
- "Is there anything going on in the world, or in your life, that you've been thinking about lately — even if it has nothing to do with school?"

---

## Find the person

The project requires him to talk to a real person about a challenge they face. That person needs to be someone he can actually have a conversation with — a family member, neighbor, family friend, or someone else he sees regularly. Help him identify who that is.

- "Is there someone in your life — family, a neighbor, anyone you know — who has a problem or a challenge that you've noticed?"
- "Is there anyone in your family who does something hard, or who struggles with something, that you've always wondered about?"
- "Think about the adults in your life. Is there someone whose job sounds interesting to you, or who deals with something you'd want to learn more about?"

If he's stuck, offer some low-stakes examples to prime the pump:
- "Like, maybe your dad has a hard time with something at work — or your mom worries about something — or someone you know has a health thing, or a money thing, or a job thing."
- "It doesn't have to be a huge world problem. It can be something small and real."

**Write down the name (or relationship) of the person he identifies.**

---

## Find the challenge

Once you have a person, dig into what that person's challenge actually is.

- "What is the hard thing for [person]? What do they struggle with, or worry about?"
- "Have you ever seen [person] get frustrated or stressed about something? What was it?"
- "If you could explain [person's] problem to someone else, how would you describe it?"

The challenge should be something that:
- Is real and specific (not "life is hard")
- Has information available online (not purely personal/emotional)
- Could plausibly be addressed by a Canva poster or infographic — meaning it's something someone could learn more about

**Write down a one-sentence description of the challenge.**

---

## Check the fit for Canva

The project ends with him building something in Canva to help inform his person about the challenge. Make sure the topic lends itself to that.

Good fits:
- A health condition someone in the family has been diagnosed with
- A problem in a neighborhood or community (traffic, safety, environment)
- A challenge related to someone's job or industry
- A topic where there are facts, statistics, or research he could summarize visually

Harder fits (steer away):
- Purely emotional or relationship problems (not much to research or visualize)
- Topics so broad they're hard to contain (e.g., "climate change" without a more specific angle)
- Topics where the "audience" wouldn't realistically benefit from an infographic

---

## Lock it in

Before ending the conversation, confirm both decisions out loud and have him repeat them back.

- "So you're going to talk to [person] about [challenge]. Does that sound right?"
- "And by the end of the project, you'll make something in Canva to help [person] understand more about [challenge]. Does that make sense?"

If something feels off — the topic is too vague, the person is inaccessible, or he doesn't seem genuinely interested — this is the time to redirect. It's much easier to change now than after he's done two checkpoints.
