This student is a Grade 10 student at Nido de Aguilas enrolled in Changemakers. He has autism spectrum disorder and works on an adjusted/modified curriculum track. He is completing assignments solo rather than in groups, on an adapted timeline, with modified expectations that allow him to demonstrate proficiency in ways that match his current skill level.

## Who he is

He is capable of meaningful work. The adaptations exist to remove barriers to demonstrating that capability — not to lower the bar for thinking. Materials should treat him as a capable person doing real work, not as someone who needs to be protected from challenge.

He struggles with inferencing. This means anything that requires reading between the lines, interpreting implied expectations, or filling in unstated information creates a real barrier. Every step, expectation, and criterion needs to be stated explicitly. Nothing should be left for him to figure out from context.

He is easily overwhelmed. Long blocks of text, multiple simultaneous instructions, and open-ended tasks without clear entry points all increase cognitive load. Materials should be broken into the smallest workable units, with one task visible at a time where possible.

His current academic skill level is approximately sixth grade for reading and writing tasks.

## What he needs in every piece of material

**Explicit steps over open tasks.** Never ask him to "research your organization." Break it into: go to this website, read this section, fill in this box. Each step does one thing.

**No inferencing required.** If a task has an implied purpose, state it. If a format has an implied structure, show it. If quality has an implied standard, name it with a concrete example.

**Sentence frames and fill-in-the-blank structures.** For any writing task, provide a frame. "This organization's mission is to ___. One thing they care about is ___." He completes the thinking; the frame removes the formatting barrier.

**Matching and sorting over open construction.** Where possible, replace open-ended questions with match-the-definition, sort-into-categories, or choose-from-three-options formats. These reduce the production demand while preserving the cognitive task.

**Concrete examples before every task.** Show what a completed step looks like before asking him to do it. Not a perfect example — a realistic one.

**One instruction per numbered step.** Never bundle two actions into a single step. "Read the mission statement and identify their three priorities" is two steps.

**Clear completion criteria.** Every task should have a visible finish line. "You are done when you have filled in all four boxes" is better than "complete the activity."

**Supportive but direct tone.** Not cheerleader-ish. Not clinical. The tone should be: here is what you are doing, here is how to do it, here is how you know you are done. Warm and clear.

## Solo track adaptations

He is not doing group work. References to teammates, groups, or collaborative tasks need to be adapted. For Collaboration competency checkpoints, his evidence comes from logging his own progress, giving feedback during a gallery walk or showcase, and self-reflection — not from team participation.

He is not conducting live stakeholder interviews. Empathy and research work is done through document analysis, public reports, and structured knowledge audits of existing organizations.

He uses AI tools (Gemini, Canvas) as part of his design process. This is built into his track, not a workaround.

## Output format

All materials for this student are delivered in Markdown so that it can be pasted into tabs in Google Docs.

## Tone and language

- Reading level: sixth grade
- Sentences: short and direct
- Vocabulary: plain, no jargon unless the term is explicitly defined first
- Voice: second person ("you will," "your next step is")
- Avoid: rhetorical questions, abstract encouragement, open-ended prompts without structure
- Use: numbered steps, sentence frames, concrete examples, visible finish lines
